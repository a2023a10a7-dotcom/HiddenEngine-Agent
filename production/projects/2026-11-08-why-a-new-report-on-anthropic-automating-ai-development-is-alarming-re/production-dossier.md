## Evidence ledger

### E1 — METR's high-access assessment (primary research source)
URL: https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-05-anthropic-ai-rd-acceleration-assessment/

- METR says its evaluation ran from **12 August to 29 October 2026** and included access to internal pretraining/RL metrics, compute information, internal-model benchmark scores, AI speedup analyses, forecasts and staff interviews.
- METR's central judgment: as of October 2026, Anthropic was likely improving model capabilities on **well-specified tasks** at about twice the rate seen from March 2024 to October 2025. METR says it would not be surprised by a true rate nearer **1.3x or 3x**.
- The AECI benchmark trend was estimated at **1.9x** the earlier rate, with a **95% confidence interval of 1.5–2.8x**.
- METR says it was confident the acceleration was not caused by faster growth in training compute.
- METR's estimate that a majority (perhaps approximately 80%) of the current acceleration is attributable to AI is explicitly a **subjective estimate with substantial uncertainty**, not a directly measured causal fraction.
- METR says AI assistance, rather than fully autonomous AI contributions, is currently the primary mechanism it was told about.
- Critical limitation: Anthropic redacted a majority of the evidence METR found most informative. METR explicitly says this limits the public's ability to judge the evidence independently.

### E2 — Anthropic's own AI R&D measurement (primary company disclosure)
URL: https://www.anthropic.com/institute/measuring-pace-of-ai-development

- Anthropic describes an internal prototype called the Anthropic R&D Automation Index.
- It uses an automation scale from AL0 (no AI involvement) to AL5 (fully autonomous, no human in the loop). AL3 means AI collaborates under close human direction; AL4 means AI leads most of a task while a human supervises.
- Anthropic reported that, as of August 2026, Claude led 26% of measured AI R&D work; more than 90% of the work was at or above the “AI collaborates” level.
- Crucially, Anthropic reported **no measured subset of AI R&D work operating fully autonomously** at that time.
- Anthropic says its measures are a prototype and notes comparability problems between labs, including lack of a common methodology and the possibility that a model evaluating its own systems may make similar errors.

## 10. Draft English narration — version 1

**Working title: When AI Starts Building Better AI**

### Scene 1 — The feedback loop (0:00–0:40)

Imagine a research team trying to build a more capable AI system. Now imagine that the tools helping that team write code, run experiments, analyze results and solve technical problems are AI systems themselves.

That creates a powerful possibility: each generation of AI could help researchers build the next one faster.

But how much of that is already happening? And are we approaching a point where AI development begins to accelerate beyond the pace humans can comfortably understand?

A new assessment inside Anthropic offers one of the clearest looks yet — and its findings come with important caveats.

### Scene 2 — Inside the investigation (0:40–1:35)

Between August 12 and October 29, 2026, the nonprofit research organization METR conducted a high-access assessment of Anthropic's AI research and development.

The researchers examined internal measurements covering model training, reinforcement learning, computing resources and benchmark performance. They also interviewed staff and reviewed analyses of how AI tools were affecting the company's research.

This matters because the public usually sees the finished model. The process that produces it — the experiments, failed approaches and engineering decisions — is mostly hidden inside the laboratory.

METR was given unusual access. But access did not mean complete public transparency: Anthropic redacted much of the evidence that METR considered most informative.

### Scene 3 — What does “twice as fast” mean? (1:35–2:40)

METR's headline result was striking.

Its analysis estimated that Anthropic's models had been improving on a set of well-specified tasks at roughly twice the earlier rate.

One measure, based on benchmark performance, showed an estimated acceleration of 1.9 times since October 2025. METR gave a 95 percent confidence interval from 1.5 to 2.8 times.

In practical terms, the organization said Anthropic appeared to have achieved roughly two years of historical benchmark progress in one year.

But this is not a claim that every AI capability has doubled, or that every real-world task is now being solved twice as quickly. The measurement concerns a defined set of tasks and a particular benchmark methodology. METR also acknowledged that alternative methodological choices can change the estimate.

### Scene 4 — The mechanism: AI helping AI research (2:40–3:55)

So what might be driving the acceleration?

METR investigated several possible factors, including compute, hiring, data and algorithmic improvements. It said the acceleration was not explained by faster growth in training compute.

Its best judgment was that AI assistance likely accounts for a majority of the current acceleration — perhaps around 80 percent. But METR emphasized that this is a subjective estimate with substantial uncertainty.

The distinction is important. AI does not need to design an entire new model independently to change the speed of research.

It can assist human researchers with coding, experiments, analysis and other tasks across the development pipeline. If those tools make each researcher more productive, the laboratory may be able to test more ideas and move through more iterations.

That is a human-led process increasingly amplified by machines.

### Scene 5 — How autonomous is it really? (3:55–5:00)

There is another important piece of evidence.

In a separate disclosure, Anthropic described how it measures the extent to which Claude performs its own research and development work.

The company said that, as of August 2026, Claude was leading 26 percent of the measured AI R&D work. More than 90 percent of the work involved AI at least as a collaborator.

But Anthropic also said that Claude was not operating fully autonomously in any measured subset of that work.

So the story is not that an AI system has already taken over the creation of its successor. The evidence points to something more gradual: AI systems are taking on larger portions of research tasks, while people still direct and supervise important parts of the process.

### Scene 6 — The transparency problem (5:00–6:10)

The most difficult question is how confidently outsiders can judge these findings.

METR says Anthropic redacted a majority of the evidence it found most informative. The company argued that revealing exact details could expose confidential information and give competitors a roadmap for faster development.

That creates a genuine tension.

Companies have reasons to protect sensitive research. But when the pace of AI development has consequences for public safety, regulators and independent researchers need enough information to evaluate what is happening.

Even METR cautions that its conclusions contain uncertainty. The benchmark trend may not capture every real-world capability, and the organization could be missing bottlenecks or other explanations.

### Scene 7 — What happens next? (6:10–7:00)

The significance of this report is not that machines have already begun independently building a new generation of themselves.

It is that the research process itself is changing.

When AI tools help researchers develop more capable AI, the speed of progress may become partly dependent on the systems being developed. That feedback loop deserves careful measurement, independent oversight and much greater transparency.

The central question is no longer simply how powerful the next model will be.

It is how much of the work required to create it will be done by AI — and whether human oversight can keep pace.
